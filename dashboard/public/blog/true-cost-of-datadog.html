The True Cost of Datadog: How Observability Billing Surprises Kill Budgets

Adrian Elder·February 14, 2026·11 min read

"Datadog pricing" is one of the most-searched terms in the entire DevOps space. That tells you something.

People don't Google "Sentry pricing" at 11pm. But they do Google "datadog pricing calculator" and "why is my datadog bill so high" — because Datadog's billing model has a way of generating genuine surprises that show up in the finance team's inbox.

This post breaks down how Datadog's pricing actually works, why bills escalate the way they do, and what a realistic monthly cost looks like for teams of different sizes. Then we'll cover the alternatives.

How Datadog Pricing Works (The Technical Reality)

Datadog doesn't sell one product. They sell a catalog of products, each with independent pricing, that you assemble into a stack. Understanding your bill requires understanding how each product is metered.

Infrastructure Monitoring: Per Host

The base of most Datadog deployments is infrastructure monitoring — the Datadog agent running on your servers.

  • Pro: $15/host/month
  • Enterprise: $23/host/month

"Per host" sounds simple until you realize what counts as a host. Virtual machines, EC2 instances, ECS tasks, GKE nodes, serverless function invocations — the definition of "host" can surprise you. Containerized environments in particular can inflate host counts significantly.

For a startup with 10 app servers: $150/month just to see your infrastructure metrics.

APM and Distributed Tracing: Per Host (Again)

APM is a separate product with separate per-host pricing on top of infrastructure:

  • APM Pro: $31/host/month
  • APM Enterprise: $40/host/month

For those same 10 servers: $310/month for APM. Combined with infrastructure, you're at $460/month before touching logs, replays, or any other product.

Log Management: Per Volume, Per Indexing Event

Logs are where Datadog bills start to get genuinely surprising.

Ingestion: $0.10/GB. Sounds cheap until you realize modern applications generate logs aggressively.

Indexing: $1.70/million log events (15-day retention) or $2.50/million log events (30-day retention). This is separate from ingestion. You pay to ingest logs AND you pay separately to make them searchable.

A busy application generating 10 million log events per day:

  • Ingestion: ~$30/month (300 GB @ $0.10/GB)
  • Indexing (15-day retention): ~$5,100/month (3 billion events @ $1.70/million)

That's $5,130/month for logs alone. This is why "datadog log cost" is its own search category.

Session Replay and RUM: Per Session

Datadog's Browser RUM (Real User Monitoring) captures user sessions.

  • RUM: $1.50/1,000 sessions
  • Session Replay: $1.80/1,000 sessions (typically added on top of RUM)

For an app with 100,000 monthly sessions: $150-330/month for RUM + replays.

Synthetic Monitoring: Per Test Run

Uptime and synthetic tests are priced per test run:

  • API tests: $5/10,000 test runs
  • Browser tests: $12/1,000 test runs

A simple setup with 50 endpoints checked every minute: 50 × 43,200 (checks/month) = 2.16 million test runs. At $5/10K = $1,080/month for uptime monitoring.

On-Call and Incident Management

Datadog acquired PagerDuty's competitor space and offers incident management. It's available in the Enterprise tier, but requires a separate commercial conversation. There's no simple self-serve pricing — it's bundled into enterprise contracts.

Building a Realistic Datadog Bill

Let's price a 20-person SaaS startup with a typical production setup:

Infrastructure:

  • 15 app servers × $15/host = $225/month
  • 5 database/cache servers × $15/host = $75/month

APM:

  • 15 app servers × $31/host = $465/month

Logs:

  • 5 million log events/day × 30 days = 150M events/month
  • Ingestion: 150 GB @ $0.10/GB = $15/month
  • Indexing (30-day): 150M events @ $2.50/million = $375/month

Session Replay:

  • 50,000 sessions/month × $1.80/1,000 = $90/month

Synthetics:

  • 20 uptime monitors, 1-minute checks = 864,000 test runs
  • 864K @ $5/10K = $432/month

Total: ~$1,677/month

And this doesn't include:

  • Datadog NPM (network performance monitoring): $5-10/host
  • Datadog Security Monitoring: additional
  • Datadog Error Tracking: additional (often bundled in APM but varies)
  • Datadog Profiling: $4/Profiled Container/month
  • Datadog Database Monitoring: $70/database/month

A fully-instrumented setup for this 20-person team realistically runs $2,000-3,500/month. That's $24,000-42,000/year.

Why Datadog Bills Spike (The Failure Modes)

Cardinality Explosions in Metrics

Datadog charges for custom metrics — and the definition of "custom metric" can catch teams off guard. A custom metric is any metric with unique tag combinations.

If you have a metric http.request.count tagged with endpoint, status_code, user_id, and region, and your app has 1,000 endpoints × 10 status codes × 10,000 active users × 5 regions — you've just created 500 million unique metric combinations. Custom metrics are priced at ~$0.05/100 metrics/month. That's $250,000/month for one poorly-tagged metric.

This is not hypothetical. Engineering teams have received six-figure Datadog bills from a single metric misconfiguration.

Log Volume Spikes

A bug that causes excessive logging, a deployment with verbose debug logging left on, or a DDoS attack that spikes request rates — all of these translate directly into Datadog log costs. There's no built-in cap. Logs flow until you notice the bill.

Auto-Scaling Hosts

If your infrastructure auto-scales (which it should), every new instance counts as a new host. A traffic spike that spins up 50 extra EC2 instances adds $750/month to your infrastructure monitoring bill for that month. Auto-scaling is designed to handle load efficiently; Datadog pricing works against this.

Free Tier → Paid Tier Transitions

Datadog offers a free trial and free tier for some features. Teams build workflows on free features, then get surprised when those features require a paid tier as usage grows. The transition is often abrupt.

The Locked-In Problem

Once a team is fully instrumented with Datadog agents, the switching cost is real. The Datadog agent is proprietary. Their SDKs use Datadog-specific tracing libraries. Getting off Datadog means:

  1. Removing Datadog agents from every server
  2. Replacing Datadog-specific tracing instrumentation
  3. Rebuilding dashboards and alert configurations
  4. Retraining the team on a new platform

This lock-in is intentional. Datadog's pricing model depends on it.

What Teams Are Doing Instead

OpenTelemetry-First Architecture

Forward-thinking teams instrument with OpenTelemetry instead of vendor-specific SDKs. OTel is vendor-neutral — you configure where telemetry is sent at the infrastructure level, not the code level. This means switching backends is a configuration change, not a re-instrumentation project.

What People Get Wrong About Datadog's Pricing

  • "We'll stay on the free tier / low tier and it'll be cheap." Datadog's pricing inflects hard once you cross the free tier boundaries. The jump from free to paid, combined with per-host-per-product metering, means your first "real" bill is often 5-10x what you expected.

  • "Per-host pricing is predictable." It's predictable for static infrastructure. The moment you adopt auto-scaling, Kubernetes, or serverless, the definition of "host" becomes fluid and your bill becomes unpredictable.

  • "Log ingestion at $0.10/GB is cheap." Ingestion is cheap. Indexing is not. The $1.70-2.50/million-events indexing fee is where the real cost hides. Teams focus on the ingestion rate and miss that searchable logs cost 10-20x more than raw ingestion.

  • "We can control costs with sampling and filtering." You can — but it requires ongoing engineering effort to maintain sampling rules, exclusion filters, and retention policies. That engineering time has a cost, and misconfigured filters create blind spots during incidents.

  • "Switching off Datadog is too painful, so we'll just optimize." Optimization helps at the margin, but it doesn't change the fundamental pricing model. A 20% reduction on a $3,000/month bill still leaves you at $2,400 — which may still be 10x what you'd pay on an alternative.

Practical Playbook: Evaluating and Reducing Your Datadog Costs

  1. Do this first: Pull your last 3 months of Datadog invoices. Break down the cost by product (infrastructure, APM, logs, RUM, synthetics, custom metrics). Identify the top 3 line items by cost.

  2. Check for log indexing costs specifically — if log indexing exceeds $200/month, you're likely a strong candidate for an alternative.

  3. Audit your host count. Compare the number of hosts Datadog reports against your actual infrastructure.

  4. Review custom metrics for cardinality explosions. Look for any metric with high-cardinality tags (user IDs, request IDs, UUIDs).

  5. Identify Datadog products you're paying for but not actively using. The catalog is wide, and teams often enable features during setup that go unused.

  6. Avoid this trap: Don't negotiate an annual contract before understanding your true usage trajectory. Committing to a spend level based on current usage locks you in if your infrastructure grows.

  7. Instrument with OpenTelemetry instead of Datadog-specific SDKs for all new services. This preserves optionality.

  8. Estimate what your stack would cost on alternatives. Use the cost scenarios in this post as a starting point, then plug in your actual numbers.

  9. If considering self-hosting, run a proof-of-concept on a single non-critical service. Send the same telemetry to both Datadog and the alternative, and compare coverage.

  10. Set billing alerts and spend caps where Datadog allows them. This won't reduce costs, but it prevents surprise invoices from runaway log volumes or metric cardinality.

  11. Factor in the cost of supplementary tools. If Datadog doesn't cover status pages or on-call, add those vendor costs to your true Datadog total.

Tradeoffs, Caveats, and Second-Order Effects

  • Datadog's breadth is genuinely hard to replicate. If you use Datadog's CI Visibility, Security Monitoring, Database Monitoring, and Network Performance Monitoring in addition to core observability, finding a single replacement that covers all of those is difficult.

  • Enterprise negotiation can reduce costs significantly. Companies spending $5,000+/month can typically negotiate 20-40% discounts with annual commitments.

  • Migration from Datadog is non-trivial. Datadog's proprietary agents and SDKs create real lock-in. Budget 2-4 weeks of engineering time for a full migration.

  • Datadog's hiring pipeline is a signal. Large companies choose Datadog partly because new hires are likely to already know it.

  • Self-hosted alternatives trade money for operational responsibility. You save on SaaS fees but take on infrastructure management.

  • Usage-based pricing aligns incentives poorly. You pay more precisely when your application is under stress — the moments when you most need your observability tools and least want to worry about cost.

A Word on Negotiating with Datadog

Datadog has significant negotiating flexibility, especially for annual commitments. If you're spending $2,000+/month, you can likely negotiate 20-40% discounts with an annual contract and committed spend. Enterprise contracts routinely include discounts, dedicated support, and rate lock-ins.

But negotiating doesn't change the fundamental pricing model — it just makes the per-unit costs slightly lower. You still pay per host, per GB, per million events. The billing unpredictability remains.

Summary

Datadog's pricing model has three characteristics that make it expensive and unpredictable:

  1. Per-product pricing means the bill grows with every feature you adopt
  2. Per-host and per-volume metering means the bill grows with your infrastructure
  3. Lack of spend caps means a misconfiguration or traffic spike translates directly to an unexpected invoice

For teams at the scale where Datadog's premium makes sense (large enterprises with dedicated platform engineering, negotiated contracts, and complex observability needs), Datadog is genuinely powerful.

For everyone else — startups, growth-stage companies, mid-market engineering teams — the cost-to-value ratio is poor. The observability stack you need exists for a fraction of the price, either through simpler SaaS alternatives or self-hosted open source platforms.

The observability category has matured. You no longer need to pay Datadog prices to get Datadog-quality observability.
