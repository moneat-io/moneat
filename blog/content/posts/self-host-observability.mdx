## The Case for Self-Hosted Observability

### 1. Data Residency and Compliance

This is the #1 driver for organizations that switch to self-hosted observability.

When you send events to Sentry's cloud or Datadog's cloud, you're sending potentially sensitive data — stack traces, user IDs, request payloads, session recordings — to servers you don't control. For most apps, this is fine. For some, it's a hard blocker.

**GDPR (EU):** Under GDPR, personal data must be processed in ways compliant with EU law. Sending EU user data to a US-based SaaS vendor requires Standard Contractual Clauses (SCCs) and careful data processing agreements. With self-hosting, EU user data stays in your EU data center, full stop. No SCCs, no DPA negotiations, no legal gray areas.

**HIPAA (Healthcare):** Any SaaS vendor that touches PHI (Protected Health Information) must sign a Business Associate Agreement (BAA). Many SaaS observability vendors either won't sign BAAs, charge a premium for it, or restrict what you can send. Self-hosting eliminates this friction — you control the data pipeline, so PHI never leaves your infrastructure.

**SOC 2 / ISO 27001:** Auditors increasingly scrutinize third-party data processors. Every SaaS vendor in your observability chain is a sub-processor that needs to be inventoried, assessed, and documented. Fewer external vendors = simpler audits.

**Data residency regulations (finance, government):** In some jurisdictions (Germany's BDSG, India's PDPB, financial services regulations), data must physically reside within national borders. Self-hosting is often the only viable path.

### 2. Cost Control at Scale

SaaS observability pricing is optimized for vendor margins, not your budget. The more data you generate, the more you pay — often exponentially.

Let's look at what happens when a typical growth-stage startup scales:

| Monthly errors | Sentry (Team plan) | Self-hosted Moneat |
|---|---|---|
| 50,000 | $29 (included) | $0 (infra costs only) |
| 500,000 | ~$115 (overages) | $0 |
| 5,000,000 | ~$1,040 (overages) | $0 |
| 50,000,000 | ~$10,000+ | $0 |

Sentry's $0.000188/error overage rate sounds trivial. It isn't. At 50M errors/month (common for high-traffic consumer apps), you're paying over $10,000/month just for error tracking. That's $120,000/year for a product your engineers could run on $500/month of infrastructure.

**The SaaS markup on observability data is enormous.** The underlying infrastructure to store and query 50M events/month costs a fraction of what vendors charge. Self-hosting captures that arbitrage.

### 3. Operational Control and Customization

Self-hosted observability lets you do things that SaaS platforms simply won't:

- **Custom retention policies** — keep security-relevant logs for 7 years without paying per-GB storage fees
- **Custom alerting integrations** — wire alerts into internal systems without webhook limitations
- **Custom data pipelines** — normalize, redact, or enrich events before storage
- **Air-gapped deployments** — run observability in environments with no internet access (defense, financial, classified environments)
- **Custom dashboards** — use your own Grafana instance with full query access to the underlying database

## The Real Tradeoffs (Being Honest)

Self-hosting isn't free. Let's address the actual costs and complexity:

### Infrastructure and Ops Overhead

Running a production-grade observability stack requires:

- A server or cluster to run the application
- A database (PostgreSQL for metadata, ClickHouse for high-volume events)
- Redis for caching and queuing
- Backups, monitoring of the monitoring system, and occasional upgrades

For small teams, this overhead is real. If you have one DevOps engineer who's already stretched thin, adding another service to maintain has a cost.

**Mitigation:** Modern self-hosted observability platforms (including Moneat) deploy via Docker Compose in under 15 minutes and require minimal day-to-day maintenance. Upgrades are a `docker-compose pull && docker-compose up -d`.

### No Managed SLA

With Sentry or Datadog, uptime is someone else's problem. With self-hosted, it's yours.

**Mitigation:** Run on a managed cloud provider (AWS, GCP, DigitalOcean) with automated backups. Most teams deploy with at least two instances. If your internal observability goes down for 30 minutes, you'll survive — it's not the application itself.

### Feature Lag vs. SaaS Vendors

SaaS platforms ship features quickly. Self-hosted open source projects sometimes lag.

**Mitigation:** Choose a self-hosted platform with an active development team and frequent releases. Moneat ships updates weekly and maintains full Sentry SDK compatibility, so the feature surface grows without requiring re-instrumentation.

## What People Get Wrong About Self-Hosting Observability

- **"Self-hosting requires a dedicated DevOps team."** Modern observability platforms deploy via Docker Compose in under 15 minutes. Day-to-day maintenance is a `docker-compose pull && docker-compose up -d` for upgrades. A team with one engineer comfortable with Docker can manage this.

- **"The infrastructure costs will eat the savings."** A $40/month VPS handles millions of events per day. Even at $200/month for a production-grade server, you're spending a fraction of what Sentry or Datadog charge at equivalent scale.

- **"Self-hosted means you lose session replay and advanced features."** This was true of older open-source observability tools. Modern platforms like Moneat include full session replay (web + mobile), distributed tracing, LLM observability, and status pages in the self-hosted edition — the same feature set as the cloud version.

- **"You need to re-instrument your entire codebase."** Not if the self-hosted platform supports your existing SDKs. Sentry-compatible self-hosted platforms accept data from existing Sentry SDKs with only a DSN change.

- **"Self-hosted observability is less reliable than SaaS."** Your self-hosted observability stack going down for 30 minutes is annoying but not catastrophic — it's the monitoring system, not the production application. SaaS vendors have outages too. The difference is that with self-hosting, you can fix it yourself instead of waiting on a vendor's status page.

## Practical Playbook: Self-Hosting Your Observability Stack

1. **Do this first:** Calculate your current monthly observability spend across all SaaS vendors. If it exceeds $500/month and growing, self-hosting likely has positive ROI within 30 days.

2. Choose a self-hosted platform that supports your existing SDK instrumentation. Re-instrumentation is the biggest hidden cost of switching — avoid it entirely by choosing a Sentry-compatible or OpenTelemetry-compatible backend.

3. Provision a server with at least 4 CPU / 8 GB RAM for production use. DigitalOcean, Hetzner, or any VPS provider works. Budget $40-80/month for a server that handles millions of events/day.

4. Deploy using Docker Compose. Follow the platform's setup guide — the process should take under 15 minutes for the initial deployment.

5. Configure automated backups for PostgreSQL (metadata) and ClickHouse (event data). Use your cloud provider's snapshot feature or a cron job with `pg_dump`.

6. **Avoid this trap:** Don't skip SSL configuration. Observability data includes stack traces, user context, and potentially sensitive information. Always deploy behind HTTPS with a valid certificate (Let's Encrypt via Caddy is the simplest path).

7. Migrate one non-critical service first. Update the DSN, trigger a test error, and verify it appears in the self-hosted dashboard.

8. Set up monitoring for your monitoring system. A simple external uptime check (even a free one from a third-party service) ensures you know if your self-hosted observability platform goes down.

9. Configure retention policies based on your compliance requirements. Self-hosting gives you full control — keep security-relevant logs for years while aggressively pruning noisy debug logs.

10. Document the deployment for your team. Include the server location, how to access the dashboard, how to run upgrades, and who to contact if the monitoring system itself has issues.

11. Schedule monthly upgrades. Pull the latest images and restart. Most platforms ship weekly updates; monthly upgrades keep you current without excessive overhead.

## What a Modern Self-Hosted Observability Stack Looks Like

A production-ready self-hosted observability stack in 2026 covers:

### Error Tracking
Captures exceptions, stack traces, and user context. Sentry-compatible SDKs work with any Sentry-compatible self-hosted backend, meaning you don't rewrite a line of instrumentation code.

### Session Replays
Records user sessions (with privacy controls to mask PII) for debugging UX issues. Self-hosted replays mean replay data — which contains sensitive user interactions — never leaves your infrastructure.

### Logs
Structured log ingestion via OTLP or syslog. ClickHouse makes querying billions of log lines fast and cheap. At self-hosted scale, log ingestion costs are essentially zero beyond storage.

### Distributed Tracing / APM
Full trace context across microservices. OpenTelemetry-compatible backends let you use standard OTel SDKs without vendor lock-in.

### Uptime Monitoring
HTTP, TCP, DNS, SSL certificate checks. Alerts when services go down. Simple to run from any public VPS as an external check.

### Infrastructure Metrics
Container health, CPU, memory, disk, network. Prometheus-compatible exporters feed into your self-hosted stack.

### Status Pages
Public-facing status pages communicating service health to users. Often overlooked in the "observability stack" framing, but essential for customer trust.

### On-Call and Incident Management
Alert routing, escalation policies, on-call schedules. Self-hosted here means your paging infrastructure doesn't depend on a third party staying online when you have an outage.

## When Self-Hosting Makes Sense (and When It Doesn't)

### Self-host if:
- You're spending > $500/month on SaaS observability and growth is projected
- You have GDPR, HIPAA, or data residency requirements
- You're a DevOps-capable team with at least one engineer comfortable with Docker
- You want to consolidate multiple observability vendors into one platform
- You're building a platform product where vendor lock-in in your own stack is a liability

### Stick with SaaS if:
- You have a small team with no DevOps bandwidth
- You're in early-stage (< $10K MRR) and infrastructure simplicity is paramount
- You're fine paying the SaaS premium in exchange for zero operational overhead

There's a middle path too: start with Moneat's cloud offering, then migrate to self-hosted when it makes financial or compliance sense. Because the SDK is the same either way, the transition requires no code changes.
